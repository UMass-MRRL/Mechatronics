Coreless DC Motor Designer

Explore an idealized ironless (coreless) brushed DC motor, similar in broad architecture to commercial coreless designs: a fixed magnet in the middle, a thin self-supporting copper winding that spins around it, and a steel housing that carries the flux back. Results come from the stated assumptions; selected dimensions are scaled, but geometry and materials are schematic. No product-specific calibration is implied.

How to use: pick a starting design, press Pin as reference, then change one knob at a time and read the % change on each value.

Good for: seeing why coreless motors have low inertia, low inductance and no cogging, and how magnet size, winding thickness and skew set kT, R and km. Voltage and current are model inputs, not limits of a particular driver.

Not for: selecting a commercial motor, inferring rated performance, or predicting real motor heating. The winding, shell, magnet, bearing and thermal representations are simplified, and the calculated mass/inertia and performance have not been validated against manufacturer data. Brand references identify motor architecture only.

Sensitivity: how this model responds at this design

Read these as properties of the model, not of real motors. They hold for changes of about ±10% from the current design. Real motors depart from them near iron saturation, temperature or voltage limits, and when several dimensions change together.

Each cell is a normalized (logarithmic) sensitivity, S = ∂ln y / ∂ln x = (x/y)(∂y/∂x). It is the local power-law exponent: if S = 2, a 1% increase in that input raises the output by about 2%. The page computes it by central finite differences (±1%) on the same equations, so it updates as you design. At stalls and mode transitions the derivative is not smooth and should not be interpreted as a local power law.

How to read it. At fixed geometry, this model makes kT proportional to winding turns N. Resistance and rotor inertia depend on several coupled geometric terms, so most sensitivity exponents vary with the design. For example, km = kT/√R implies S(km) = S(kT) − ½S(R), evaluated with respect to the same input.

Try this: Increase winding turns N while holding shell geometry and copper fill fixed. The wire area decreases, kT increases, and resistance grows strongly. Compare the sensitivities of kT, R, and km. Then switch winding style and observe how the changed turn length affects mass and rotor inertia.

Method: local, normalized sensitivity analysis. See P. M. Frank, Introduction to System Sensitivity Theory (Academic Press, 1978), and A. Saltelli et al., Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer (Wiley, 2008), ch. 1. Control-system sensitivity functions are related logarithmic derivatives, but the closed-loop transfer and perturbed quantity must be specified separately.

Try this

  1. Start from the 13 mm design. Compare its rotor inertia and inductance with the 12 V iron-core hobby motor in the DC Motor Designer. Which construction would you pick for a fast pick-and-place axis, and why?
  2. Switch the winding style from rhombic to straight. kT per turn rises by π/2, but copper length increases by a geometry-dependent factor (not necessarily π/2). What happens to km? Why do maxon and Faulhaber still skew?
  3. Make the shell thicker with the outside diameter fixed (shrink the magnet to compensate). Find the shell thickness that maximizes km. What two effects are you trading?
  4. Double the clearances gi and go. Compare the percentage drop in B with what the same change would do in an iron-core motor. Which design is more forgiving of loose tolerances?
  5. Swap NdFeB for ferrite. How much bigger must the magnet be to recover kT? Why do almost all coreless motors use rare-earth magnets?
  6. Stall the 13 mm motor and read the time to 130 °C from the thermal plot. Compare the winding time constant with the motor time constant. Why are coreless motors easy to overheat in short bursts?
  7. Load the 40 mm design and compare kT, R, L and J with the maxon RE 40 datasheet (catalog p. 163). Which numbers agree, and which missing effect best explains the rest?
Full model and assumptions
  • Construction: stationary diametrically magnetized cylinder magnet, rotating self-supporting winding shell, steel housing as the only flux return. 2 poles, a = 2 parallel paths between the brushes.
  • Field: B = Br·rm/(rw + μrgt) from flux continuity (magnet section dm·l, gap section 2rw·l per pole) and a series magnetic circuit with ideal iron. No end leakage, no armature reaction, no housing saturation.
  • Winding: rhombic (skewed) conductors sweep 180° along the active length, giving skew factor 2/π on torque and on inductance, and turn length 2√(l² + (πrw)²). At fixed shell cross-section and copper fraction, wire size is fixed; turn length sets copper/epoxy volume, shell mass, and rotor inertia. Straight style: ks = 1, turn length 2l + 2πrw.
  • Rotor inertia: shell (copper + epoxy) at rw, with equivalent shell length lturn/2 to represent wire paths and wound ends; brass hub plate 0.6 mm thick; steel shaft. Length overhead for brushes, commutator and bearings: 0.45D + 4 mm.
  • Thermal: two nodes. Shell to housing Rth1 by convection on both shell faces with hgap = 75 W/m²K; housing to ambient by natural convection (horizontal cylinder, ΔT ≈ 50 K) plus radiation. Set against Faulhaber 1336 CXR and maxon RE 40 thermal data.
  • Coupled dynamics, drive modes, gearhead and load: the same model as the DC Motor Designer. Brush contact resistance and voltage drop are ignored.
  • Quantitative calibration: this version is not calibrated to manufacturer data. Earlier comparisons against Faulhaber and maxon datasets used a previous winding-mass approximation and should not be treated as error bounds for the revised model. Use predictions for relative design trends until revalidated.
