Brushless DC Motor Designer

Explore a simplified slotless brushless DC motor architecture: a rotating magnet ring on a steel hub, a stationary skewed three-phase winding in the gap, and a laminated stator yoke. Electronics replace the brushes. Results follow the stated equivalent motor model. Selected dimensions are scaled but internal construction is schematic; no commercial product is being simulated.

How to use: pick a starting design, press Pin as reference, then change one knob at a time and read the % change on each value.

Good for: seeing how pole count, magnet, winding and yoke set kT, R, inductance and iron loss, and how block and sinusoidal commutation patterns differ visually; both use one equivalent terminal-performance model. Voltage and current are model inputs, not limits of a particular driver.

Not for: real motor selection, inverter design, torque-ripple prediction, or thermal ratings. This equivalent-motor model does not resolve three-phase inverter switches or the different torque/current ripple of six-step versus sinusoidal commutation. Iron-loss coefficients are illustrative, and predictions are not manufacturer-calibrated.

Sensitivity: how this model responds at this design

Read these as properties of the model, not of real motors. They hold for changes of about ±10% from the current design. Real motors depart from them near iron saturation, temperature or voltage limits, and when several dimensions change together.

Each cell is a normalized (logarithmic) sensitivity, S = ∂ln y / ∂ln x = (x/y)(∂y/∂x). It is the local power-law exponent: if S = 2, a 1% increase in that input raises the output by about 2%. The page computes it by central finite differences (±1%) on the same equations, so it updates as you design. At stalls and mode transitions the derivative is not smooth and should not be interpreted as a local power law.

How to read it. At fixed geometry, this model makes kT proportional to winding turns N. Resistance and rotor inertia depend on several coupled geometric terms, so most sensitivity exponents vary with the design. For example, km = kT/√R implies S(km) = S(kT) − ½S(R), evaluated with respect to the same input.

Try this: Change winding turns per phase with geometry and copper fill fixed. Compare kT, R, and km. Then reduce yoke thickness and note that an apparently favorable sensitivity may cease to have physical meaning when the unsaturated-yoke assumption fails.

Method: local, normalized sensitivity analysis. See P. M. Frank, Introduction to System Sensitivity Theory (Academic Press, 1978), and A. Saltelli et al., Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer (Wiley, 2008), ch. 1. Control-system sensitivity functions are related logarithmic derivatives, but the closed-loop transfer and perturbed quantity must be specified separately.

Try this

  1. Switch the 4-pole design to 2 poles (the third preset). kT does not change, but the yoke saturates. Why does halving the pole count double the yoke flux density? How thick must the yoke be to fix it, and what does that do to the outside diameter?
  2. Now try 8 poles. The yoke gets thinner, but iron loss at full speed rises. Find the electrical frequency at no load and explain where the extra loss comes from.
  3. Compare the no-load current of this motor with the brushed DC Motor Designer. Why does a brushless motor's no-load current grow with speed even with perfect bearings?
  4. Switch between block and sinusoidal commutation and watch which winding belts carry current. How would torque ripple differ? The steady-state model deliberately remains unchanged, so this is a visualization, not a computed performance comparison.
  5. Compare the winding-to-housing thermal resistance Rth1 with the coreless designer. Why can a brushless motor run its copper harder for the same size?
  6. Change the 48 V design to the 18 V winding. Which constants change, by what ratio, and which stay fixed? Check against the four windings on maxon catalog page 293 (EC-4pole 30, 100 W).
  7. Make the winding thicker with the outside diameter fixed (shrink the magnet). Find the thickness that maximizes km. What two effects are you trading?
Full model and assumptions
  • Construction: inner rotor with a magnet ring (p pole pairs) on a steel hub; stationary slotless winding bonded to a laminated yoke; steel housing. Star-connected three-phase winding with 60° phase belts.
  • Field: B = Brhm/(hm + μr(g + tw)), assumed sinusoidal around the gap. No leakage, no armature reaction, ideal hub and yoke iron below saturation.
  • Constants: per-phase back-EMF amplitude ê = 2kwNphB rw l ω. Block commutation: kT = kv = (3√3/π)kwNphB l (2rw), terminal R = 2Rph, terminal L = 3Lself. Winding factor kw = kdks, distribution factor sin 30°/(π/6) and skew factor 2/π for the rhombic winding.
  • Yoke loss: a simplified Steinmetz estimate with kh = 0.012 W/(kg·Hz·T²) and ke = 1.0×10⁻⁴ W/(kg·Hz²·T²). A constant hysteresis torque would incorrectly create a static breakaway threshold, so the mechanical model approximates it as viscous drag matched at 1000 rad/s: bh = τh/(1000 rad/s). This is a local linearization, not an exact hysteresis model at other speeds; eddy loss remains viscous. Magnet/winding eddy currents are ignored.
  • Drive: the DC bus is applied through an ideal inverter, without switch drops or dead time. Block and sinusoidal drive share one equivalent terminal model; commutation choice changes only the animation and displayed sinusoidal constant, NOT the modeled performance or torque ripple.
  • Winding copper mass is calculated from 3Nph conductor turns times wire area times style-dependent turn length; epoxy uses the same effective shell volume at fixed fill. This is an equivalent-volume geometric approximation, not a detailed end-turn model.
  • Thermal: two nodes. Winding to housing through half the winding thickness with keff = 0.4 W/m·K; housing to ambient by natural convection plus radiation. Copper loss heats the winding; iron and bearing loss heat the stator and housing.
  • Quantitative calibration: winding mass and thermal capacitance were recomputed from wire length in this version, so numerical agreement reported for an earlier geometry version is no longer a valid calibration result. Compare independently with the manufacturer's datasheets before using absolute predictions.
