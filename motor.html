DC Motor Designer

Change the winding, geometry, magnets, gearing, and load of a brushed DC motor. Outputs follow the documented idealized equations. Click a component or calculated value to inspect the model. Scale bars represent selected dimensions; internal construction and proportions are schematic, not manufacturing drawings.

How to use: pick a starting design, press Pin as reference, then change one knob at a time and read the % change on each value.

Good for: theoretical exploration of how winding, geometry, magnet properties, voltage, current control, gearing, and load affect motor performance. Voltage and current are model inputs, not limits of a particular driver.

Not for: rated power, safe stall duration, component selection, or predictions for a specific purchased motor. This idealized model omits important saturation, leakage, brush-contact, commutation, and temperature-coupled effects. Presets represent approximate sizes and assumptions, not calibrated products.

Sensitivity: how this model responds at this design

Read these as properties of the model, not of real motors. They hold for changes of about ±10% from the current design. Real motors depart from them near iron saturation, temperature or voltage limits, and when several dimensions change together.

Each cell is a normalized (logarithmic) sensitivity, S = ∂ln y / ∂ln x = (x/y)(∂y/∂x). It is the local power-law exponent: if S = 2, a 1% increase in that input raises the output by about 2%. The page computes it by central finite differences (±1%) on the same equations, so it updates as you design. At stalls and mode transitions the derivative is not smooth and should not be interpreted as a local power law.

How to read it. Exact power laws show up as whole numbers: kT ∝ N B l d gives 1.00 for N and d, and Jrotor ∝ l d⁴ gives 4.00 for d. Outputs built from sums are not pure power laws, so their exponents are local and change as you design. Exponents of a product add: for the frictionless datasheet definition τm = R J / kT², S(τm) = S(R) + S(J) − 2 S(kT).

Example, default 12 V design, input d: S(R) = 0.39 (wire length grows with 2l + 2d), S(J) = 0.20 (the rotor's d⁴ is diluted because the flywheel is 95% of the inertia), S(kT) = 1.00. Thus, for frictionless τm, S = 0.39 + 0.20 − 2.00 = −1.41. A 10% larger rotor predicts τm × 1.1−1.41 = 0.874; the full model gives 0.878. Remove the flywheel and the same row turns positive, because the rotor's own d⁴ inertia then dominates.

Method: local, normalized sensitivity analysis. See P. M. Frank, Introduction to System Sensitivity Theory (Academic Press, 1978), and A. Saltelli et al., Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer (Wiley, 2008), ch. 1. Control-system sensitivity functions are related logarithmic derivatives, but the closed-loop transfer and perturbed quantity must be specified separately.

Try this

  1. Double the rotor diameter d. Before you look: what happens to kT, to rotor inertia, and to the two mechanical time-constant approximations? Check the sensitivity table.
  2. Double N with the same wire. Stall torque barely changes. Why? Now also shrink the wire so the slot fill stays the same. What happens to km = kT/√R?
  3. Open the air gap from 0.5 mm to 2 mm. Which numbers suffer most? Compare ferrite with NdFeB at equal magnet thickness: why is the percentage change in B identical in this simplified magnetic-circuit model even though their absolute fields differ?
  4. Set the load torque to zero, pin the design, and sweep the gear ratio while watching approximate startup acceleration. Compare the peak to √(JL/Jrotor). Add opposing load torque. Does the maximum shift as the gearhead formula predicts?
  5. Set winding temperature to 120 °C. Which constants change and by how much? This is why a motor that runs hot gets weaker.
  6. Raise τL until the motor stalls. Look at copper loss and the temperature estimate. What would a current limit in the driver buy you?
  7. Switch to current mode and command half the voltage-mode stall current. Compare the step responses. Why is the current-mode speed a ramp, and what sets where the ramp bends over?
  8. Read the steady-state temperature from the thermal plot, then set the T knob to that value. R rises and the magnets weaken. Does the new steady state move up or down? Repeat until it stops changing. That iterative estimate illustrates the electro-thermal coupling this page leaves out; it does not replace a coupled thermal simulation.
  9. Stall the motor and observe the approximate time for the lumped motor temperature to reach the illustrative 130 °C threshold. Why is this not a validated safe stall time for a real motor?
  10. Load the N20-sized design and compare its predicted no-load speed and stall current with what you measured in A4.
Full model and assumptions
  • Drive modes. Voltage: the full em is across the motor. Current: an ideal current-controlled driver holds i = icmd as long as R i + kvω ≤ em; past that it saturates at em. The current loop is assumed instantaneous, and driver losses and PWM ripple are ignored.
  • Time constants and dynamics: τe = L/R, τeff = J/(b+kT²/R) for L/R small, and frictionless datasheet τm = RJ/kT². Voltage drive uses the full second-order model with zero initial current. Signed current is retained, so inductive overshoot can briefly generate negative (regenerative) terminal current; heat uses i²R. Current mode assumes ideal instantaneous regulation and switches to voltage-limited operation at high speed.
  • Coupled model: L di/dt + R i + kvω = em, J dω/dt + bω = kT i − τL,motor. SI units, so kv = kT.
  • 2-pole stator, radial gap field Br = B cos φ. N total turns split evenly over m coils in a closed winding. With m ≥ 3 the brushes split it into a = 2 parallel paths, each carrying i/2 (a = 1 for a single coil). Averaging over angle gives kT = (2/π) N B l d / a, the textbook K = ZPΦ/(2πa) for this field shape.
  • Gap flux from a series magnetic circuit with infinitely permeable iron: B = Br hm / (hm + μr g). Ignores leakage, armature reaction, and iron saturation, so real B is lower.
  • Resistance from copper wire length, one turn ≈ 2l + 2d, divided by a² for the parallel paths. Brush contact resistance and voltage drop are ignored, which makes small motors look better than they are. Copper ρ = 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 20 °C, +0.393 %/°C. Magnets lose remanence with temperature (ferrite −0.20 %/°C, NdFeB −0.12 %/°C), assumed at winding temperature.
  • Inductance from the same magnetic circuit: L = μ0N² l d / (2a²(g + hm/μr)). An upper-end estimate.
  • Gearhead inertia, shaft inertia, cogging and torque ripple are left out of the dynamics. Ripple is shown in the drawing only.
  • Rotor is a solid cylinder (ρ = 7800 kg/m³). Flywheel is a steel disk (ρ = 7850 kg/m³). Gearhead reflects load inertia by 1/Ng² and opposing load torque by 1/(Ngηg). This simplified gear loss is applied only to external load torque, not to acceleration torque. Reverse rotation and back-driving are excluded.
  • Thermal model: two nodes. Winding node (copper + rotor iron) heated by i²R; housing node (housing + magnets) heated by bearing friction bω². Winding to housing Rth1 = 1/(hgap·πd(l + d)) with hgap = 40 W/m²K, set from the Pittman DC040B datasheet; housing to ambient Rth2 = 1/(h × outer surface) with h = 1.32(50 K/D)¼ + 6 W/m²K (natural convection of a horizontal cylinder at ΔT ≈ 50 K, plus radiation). Heat capacities use steel 460, copper 385, ferrite 800, NdFeB 440 J/kg·K. Electrical properties are evaluated at the selected T and are not updated as the winding heats. Both nodes start at the selected T with 25 °C ambient. This is not a validated winding temperature model or a safe-stall-time calculation.
  • Slot fill is copper conductor area 2NAw divided by the assumed slot area, 45% of the rotor cross-section. Values above 100% are geometrically impossible in this approximation; values above about 55–60% of actual slot area are difficult in practice.
