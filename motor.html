DC Motor Designer

Change the winding, geometry, magnets, gearing, and load of a brushed DC motor. Every number and every curve on this page comes from the equations shown when you tap a part. The drawings are to scale.

How to use: pick a starting design, press Pin as reference, then change one knob at a time and read the % change on each value.

Good for: seeing which design choices matter and why, comparing two designs, and checking your own hand calculations from the lecture equations.

Not for: predicting a real motor's numbers. Real motors have iron that saturates, flux leakage, brush and iron losses, and they heat up. They make less torque and run hotter than this page shows.

Sensitivity: how this model responds at this design

Read these as properties of the model, not of real motors. They hold for changes of about ±10% from the current design. Real motors depart from them near iron saturation, temperature or voltage limits, and when several dimensions change together.

Each cell is a normalized (logarithmic) sensitivity, S = ∂ln y / ∂ln x = (x/y)(∂y/∂x). It is the local power-law exponent: if S = 2, a 1% increase in that input raises the output by about 2%. The page computes it by central finite differences (±1%) on the same equations, so it updates as you design.

How to read it. Exact power laws show up as whole numbers: kT ∝ N B l d gives 1.00 for N and d, and Jrotor ∝ l d⁴ gives 4.00 for d. Outputs built from sums are not pure power laws, so their exponents are local and change as you design. Exponents of a product add: since τm = R J / kT², S(τm) = S(R) + S(J) − 2 S(kT).

Example, default 12 V design, input d: S(R) = 0.39 (wire length grows with 2l + 2d), S(J) = 0.20 (the rotor's d⁴ is diluted because the flywheel is 95% of the inertia), S(kT) = 1.00. So S(τm) = 0.39 + 0.20 − 2.00 = −1.41. A 10% larger rotor predicts τm × 1.1−1.41 = 0.874; the full model gives 0.878. Remove the flywheel and the same row turns positive, because the rotor's own d⁴ inertia then dominates.

Method: local, normalized sensitivity analysis. See P. M. Frank, Introduction to System Sensitivity Theory (Academic Press, 1978), and A. Saltelli et al., Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer (Wiley, 2008), ch. 1. It is the same quantity as Bode's sensitivity of a closed-loop transfer function, S = (dT/T)/(dG/G).

Try this

  1. Double the rotor diameter d. Before you look: what happens to kT, to rotor inertia, and to the mechanical time constant? Check the scaling table.
  2. Double N with the same wire. Stall torque barely changes. Why? Now also shrink the wire so the slot fill stays the same. What happens to km = kT/√R?
  3. Open the air gap from 0.5 mm to 2 mm. Which numbers suffer most, and why does a ferrite design suffer more than NdFeB with the same magnet thickness?
  4. Pin a design, then sweep the gear ratio and watch Initial output acceleration. Where does it peak? Compare that ratio with √(JL/Jrotor) shown on the gearhead. Why does τm keep falling past that point even though the output accelerates less?
  5. Set winding temperature to 120 °C. Which constants change and by how much? This is why a motor that runs hot gets weaker.
  6. Raise τL until the motor stalls. Look at copper loss and the temperature estimate. What would a current limit in the driver buy you?
  7. Switch to current mode and command half the voltage-mode stall current. Compare the step responses. Why is the current-mode speed a ramp, and what sets where the ramp bends over?
  8. Read the steady-state temperature from the thermal plot, then set the T knob to that value. R rises and the magnets weaken. Does the new steady state move up or down? Repeat until it stops changing. That loop is the electro-thermal coupling this page leaves out.
  9. Stall the motor and read how long the winding takes to reach 130 °C. Compare that with τm. Why can a stall survive a few seconds but not a few minutes?
  10. Load the N20-sized design and compare its predicted no-load speed and stall current with what you measured in A4.
Full model and assumptions
  • Drive modes. Voltage: the full em is across the motor. Current: an ideal current-controlled driver holds i = icmd as long as R i + kvω ≤ em; past that it saturates at em. The current loop is assumed much faster than τm, and driver losses and PWM ripple are ignored.
  • Coupled model: L di/dt + R i + kvω = em, J dω/dt + bω = kT i − τL,motor. SI units, so kv = kT.
  • 2-pole stator, radial gap field Br = B cos φ. N total turns split evenly over m coils in a closed winding. With m ≥ 3 the brushes split it into a = 2 parallel paths, each carrying i/2 (a = 1 for a single coil). Averaging over angle gives kT = (2/π) N B l d / a, the textbook K = ZPΦ/(2πa) for this field shape.
  • Gap flux from a series magnetic circuit with infinitely permeable iron: B = Br hm / (hm + μr g). Ignores leakage, armature reaction, and iron saturation, so real B is lower.
  • Resistance from copper wire length, one turn ≈ 2l + 2d, divided by a² for the parallel paths. Brush contact resistance and voltage drop are ignored, which makes small motors look better than they are. Copper ρ = 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 20 °C, +0.393 %/°C. Magnets lose remanence with temperature (ferrite −0.20 %/°C, NdFeB −0.12 %/°C), assumed at winding temperature.
  • Inductance from the same magnetic circuit: L = μ0N² l d / (2a²(g + hm/μr)). An upper-end estimate.
  • Gearhead inertia, shaft inertia, cogging and torque ripple are left out of the dynamics. Ripple is shown in the drawing only.
  • Rotor is a solid cylinder (ρ = 7800 kg/m³). Flywheel is a steel disk (ρ = 7850 kg/m³). Gearhead reflects load inertia by 1/Ng² and load torque by 1/(Ngηg). Load torque does not back-drive the motor.
  • Thermal model: one lumped node, Cth dT/dt = i²R + bω² − (T − 25 °C)/Rth. Rth is still-air convection (h ≈ 12 W/m²K) from the housing surface. Cth sums mass × specific heat of rotor and housing steel (460 J/kg·K), copper (385), and magnets (ferrite 800, NdFeB 440). The losses use constants at the temperature set by the T knob; they are not updated as the motor heats. Use it to compare designs and time scales, not to predict a winding temperature.
  • Slot fill compares copper area 2NAw with 45% of the rotor cross-section. Hand-wound round wire rarely exceeds about 50% fill.
